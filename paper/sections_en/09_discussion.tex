\section{Discussion}

The evidence supports a focused claim: KCH-MedRank provides a knowledge-constrained learning-to-rank framework that improves biomedical evidence retrieval over strong retrieval and semantic reranking baselines. The strongest statistically supported comparison against the true MedCPT Cross-Encoder baseline is Recall@10; MRR@10 and nDCG@10 move in the right direction but are not significant. Therefore, the safest interpretation is that KCH-MedRank is particularly useful for moving additional relevant evidence into the top-10 evidence set, rather than for guaranteeing better first-rank precision in every query.

An important scope constraint emerges from the structural ablation. The flat knowledge LTR baseline, which removes graph and hypergraph structure but keeps retrieval, biomedical semantic, MeSH, entity, and relation features, is not significantly worse than the full model at $k{=}10$. This means the paper's main performance claim should be understood as a knowledge-constrained learning-to-rank result rather than as proof that graph structure is the dominant driver. The hypergraph topology also does not provide a statistically significant advantage over pairwise graph decomposition at $k{=}10$ (Comparison 5). It provides modest, targeted benefits at early ranks ($k{=}5$) and in specific question types — most notably questions lacking direct MeSH overlap between query and gold evidence ($\Delta_{\text{Recall@5}} = +0.0098$, $p{=}0.006$).

The ablations suggest that the method should be understood as a combined biomedical reranking framework. Retrieval and semantic features remain strong contributors, while MeSH hierarchy, entity overlap, relation features, and graph or hypergraph diffusion provide complementary signals. The structural ablation in Section~7 demonstrates this complementarity directly: the local hypergraph without medical knowledge underperforms the flat knowledge baseline, but adding medical knowledge constraints reverses the structure-induced degradation relative to the no-medical-knowledge hypergraph. This is scientifically useful but also constrains the novelty claim: the contribution is not a standalone hypergraph formula, but a reproducible way to combine biomedical knowledge features with optional local structural features for query-level reranking.

Additional selection diagnostics support keeping this interpretation conservative. A supplemental run that selected the full KCH-MedRank model primarily by validation Recall@10 reached held-out Recall@10 of 0.5294, below the reported validation-MRR-selected main model at 0.5329. Therefore, the paper retains the original validation policy rather than retuning to the test objective after observing held-out results. This diagnostic also suggests that simply changing the selection priority is insufficient; future gains are more likely to come from better biomedical concept normalization and more stable feature-group selection.

The PubMedQA Qwen3-8B pilot is kept as an appendix-style downstream RAG-system diagnostic. Under fixed evidence count, prompt, model, and decoding settings, KCH-style hypergraph evidence improves answer accuracy and Macro-F1 over Hybrid RRF evidence on 100 PubMedQA questions. The citation-support rate is already high for all methods, so the pilot should not be read as proof of better faithfulness. It shows that white-box evidence selection can influence a downstream medical RAG answer loop without training or fine-tuning a large model, but it remains too small and incomplete to support a primary answer-generation claim.

Several limitations remain. First, the method depends on a strong candidate pool; if top-100 recall is low, reranking cannot recover missing evidence. Second, exact-name PrimeKG mapping is sparse and should not be overclaimed. Third, the flat knowledge LTR baseline is highly competitive with the full model, so graph and hypergraph features should be positioned as interpretable complementary signals rather than dominant performance drivers. Fourth, the hypergraph topology does not significantly outperform the pairwise graph decomposition at $k{=}10$; the hypergraph should therefore be positioned as a beneficial architectural variant with advantages concentrated in early-rank positions and questions requiring indirect knowledge pathways. Fifth, strong-ablation bootstrap tests show that some differences between full KCH-MedRank and close ablations are not significant, so MeSH hierarchy, entity, and relation signals should be described as complementary rather than dominant in isolation. Sixth, the NFCorpus experiment is retrieval-only and uses only retrieval features, so it supports robustness of supervised reranking but not full transfer of the BioASQ knowledge-constrained feature stack. Seventh, the Qwen3-8B generation result is a 100-question PubMedQA pilot; it should be scaled and repeated with a completed biomedical cross-encoder condition before being used as a central answer-generation claim. Eighth, the three-mechanism feature-attribution analysis (Section~8) uses the same feature set that the LambdaMART ranker consumes; it identifies which knowledge features are most strongly activated during rescues, not which features causally drive the ranking. Future work with model-agnostic attribution methods (e.g., SHAP) could strengthen the causal interpretability claim. Ninth, the hyperparameter sensitivity analysis (Section~5.5) measures diffusion-score correlations on a 30-question sample; full retrieval-metric stability under the complete LambdaMART training pipeline remains to be systematically evaluated.

\subsection{Computational Profile}

\input{tables/efficiency_analysis}

Table~\ref{tab:efficiency-analysis} summarizes the computational profile available from the experiment logs. The local hypergraph is built only over the top-$M$ candidates for each question rather than over a global biomedical corpus, so graph construction and diffusion scale with the candidate pool and local feature coverage. In the enhanced BioASQ setting, the final learning-to-rank matrix has 45 features and 377,600 train-plus-validation rows, which keeps reranking within a conventional tabular ranking regime. Dense biomedical retrieval benefits from GPU acceleration. Cross-encoder rescoring is substantially more expensive: an early CPU run with a generic cross-encoder timed out after 2,400 seconds, while the final MedCPT Cross-Encoder baseline required a separate controlled full test run.

\input{tables/reranking_efficiency}

Table~\ref{tab:reranking-efficiency} reports a controlled reranking-stage timing comparison on the held-out BioASQ test split. All rows use the same enhanced top-100 candidate pool over 943 questions and 94,300 candidate passages. The comparison excludes first-stage candidate generation, one-time model loading, and offline LambdaMART training, and it explicitly separates whether Cross-Encoder scores are used and whether Cross-Encoder scoring is run online. MedCPT Cross-Encoder scoring on CUDA takes 699.46 seconds. KCH-MedRank uses a precomputed dense biomedical semantic feature rather than a Cross-Encoder feature; its test-time feature construction plus LightGBM scoring on CPU takes 37.59 seconds, corresponding to an 18.6$\times$ reranking-stage speedup under this accounting. The no-semantic KCH diagnostic remains competitive with MedCPT Cross-Encoder on Recall@10 (0.5244 versus 0.5172), while retrieval-feature-only LambdaMART is much faster but has lower top-10 evidence quality than full KCH-MedRank. This strengthens the practical interpretation of the main result without attributing the speed comparison to hidden Cross-Encoder features. End-to-end production benchmarking, including first-stage retrieval and data-loading overhead, remains future work.

Future work should improve biomedical concept normalization, add better synonym and abbreviation handling, and scale controlled answer generation after evidence support metrics are complete. A stronger generation study should measure citation support, unsupported claims, and answer-evidence entity consistency, not only answer accuracy.
