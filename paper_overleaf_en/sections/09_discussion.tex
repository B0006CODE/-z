\section{Discussion}

The results point to three central findings. First, KCH-MedRank improves top-10 evidence retrieval over strong baselines including a true biomedical cross-encoder. Second, flat biomedical knowledge features account for most of this gain; graph and hypergraph structure contributes as a complementary interpretable signal rather than a dominant performance driver. Third, medical knowledge constraints are necessary for hypergraph structure to be useful---without them, raw topology introduces noise.

The relationship between flat features and structural features merits specific attention. The flat knowledge LTR baseline matches the full model at $k{=}10$, which might appear to diminish the contribution of hypergraph modeling. We see the opposite: this finding isolates the role of structure with unusual clarity. The hypergraph does not improve aggregate ranking metrics by large margins because the flat features are already strong. Where it provides statistically significant benefit---at $k{=}5$, in questions without direct MeSH overlap, in multi-evidence scenarios---it does so precisely where pairwise similarity and flat concept features reach their limits. The hypergraph is not a uniformly better ranker; it is a targeted structural component whose effect is visible where indirect knowledge pathways are needed.

The ablation design itself yields a methodological contribution. By comparing the hypergraph-with-medical-knowledge against the hypergraph-without-medical-knowledge (Comparison~3), we demonstrate that medical knowledge constraints reverse structural noise into useful signal. This is a stronger test than comparing against a no-structure baseline, because it controls for the hypergraph architecture itself. Approaches that apply graph structure without domain-grounded concept annotations---a common pattern in generic GraphRAG pipelines---risk performance degradation that domain constraints can correct. The finding that MeSH hierarchy accounts for 75.9\% of rescued passages, and that 89.2\% of rescues involve multiple concurrent knowledge pathways, reinforces the value of integrated biomedical constraints over isolated structural heuristics.

The computational profile also matters for practical deployment. KCH-MedRank's test-time feature construction and LightGBM scoring (37.59~seconds on CPU for 943 questions) is 18.6$\times$ faster than MedCPT Cross-Encoder scoring (699.46~seconds on CUDA) when candidate generation and offline training are excluded (Tables~\ref{tab:efficiency-analysis} and~\ref{tab:reranking-efficiency}). This speed advantage comes from using a precomputed dense biomedical semantic feature rather than online cross-encoder scoring, and it means the full knowledge-constrained reranker can operate at practical throughput for large-scale biomedical evidence retrieval.

Several limitations merit attention. The method depends on a strong candidate pool; low top-100 recall imposes a hard ceiling. Exact-name PrimeKG mapping is sparse, limiting relation-based feature coverage. The flat knowledge LTR baseline is highly competitive, so graph and hypergraph features add value in well-defined conditions (early ranks, no-direct-overlap questions, multi-evidence scenarios) rather than uniformly. The three-mechanism interpretability analysis identifies which features activate during rescues, not which causally drive ranking decisions; future work with SHAP or similar attribution methods can strengthen the causal claim. Hyperparameter sensitivity was measured on a 30-question sample at the correlation level; full retrieval-metric stability under the complete LambdaMART pipeline remains to be systematically verified.

Future work should improve biomedical concept normalization to address the synonym and abbreviation failures evident in the lost-case analysis, scale the PubMedQA generation pilot to include a completed biomedical cross-encoder condition, and extend the NFCorpus diagnostic to include dataset-specific knowledge features. A stronger generation study should measure citation support, unsupported claims, and answer-evidence entity consistency rather than answer accuracy alone.
