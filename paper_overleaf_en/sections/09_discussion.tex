KCH-MedRank improves top-10 evidence retrieval over strong baselines including a true biomedical cross-encoder, and the ablation shows this improvement has a structure worth understanding.

The flat biomedical knowledge LTR baseline is very competitive. It matches the full model at $k{=}10$. This could look like bad news for the hypergraph. We read it the other way: the result isolates what structure contributes precisely by showing where flat features reach their limit. The hypergraph adds statistically significant benefit at $k{=}5$, in questions without direct MeSH overlap, and in multi-evidence scenarios. These are exactly the conditions where pairwise similarity and flat concept features fall short. The hypergraph is a targeted structural component. Its effect only surfaces where indirect knowledge pathways are needed.

The most important finding may be that raw hypergraph topology, applied without medical knowledge annotations, actively degrades ranking. Generic GraphRAG and HyperGraphRAG pipelines build edges from lexical overlap or embedding similarity on the assumption that graph structure is beneficial. Our controlled comparison puts this assumption to the test. We keep the hypergraph architecture identical and toggle only the medical annotations. The same structure that hurts without MeSH and entity constraints becomes a significant signal with them. Domain constraints are what make structure useful. The finding that MeSH hierarchy drives 75.9\% of rescued passages, and that 89.2\% of rescues involve multiple concurrent knowledge pathways, reinforces the case for integrated biomedical constraints rather than isolated structural heuristics.

On the practical side, the speed difference matters. KCH-MedRank's test-time feature construction and LightGBM scoring takes 37.59 seconds on CPU for 943 questions against 699.46 seconds on CUDA for the MedCPT Cross-Encoder, an $18.6\times$ gap when candidate generation and offline training are excluded (Tables~\ref{tab:efficiency-analysis} and~\ref{tab:reranking-efficiency}). The gain comes from using precomputed dense biomedical semantic features rather than online cross-encoder scoring. A full knowledge-constrained reranker operating at this throughput is practical for large-scale biomedical evidence retrieval.

Several limitations deserve acknowledgment. The method requires a strong candidate pool; low top-100 recall imposes a hard ceiling no reranker can overcome. Exact-name PrimeKG mapping remains sparse and limits relation-based feature coverage. The flat knowledge LTR baseline is highly competitive, so hypergraph features add value in specific conditions rather than uniformly. The three-mechanism interpretability analysis identifies which features activate during rescues, not which causally drive ranking decisions. SHAP or comparable attribution would strengthen the causal claim. Hyperparameter sensitivity was measured on a 30-question sample at the correlation level; full retrieval-metric stability under the complete LambdaMART pipeline has not been systematically verified.

The error analysis points to the clearest direction for future work. Biomedical concept normalization that handles synonyms and abbreviations would address the ubiquitination, thyroid hormone analog, and integrin terminology failures. Scaling the PubMedQA generation pilot to include a completed biomedical cross-encoder condition would strengthen the downstream evidence. Extending NFCorpus with dataset-specific knowledge features would give a more complete robustness picture. Downstream generation studies should measure citation support, unsupported claims, and answer-evidence entity consistency rather than stop at answer accuracy. The current PubMedQA pilot already shows a gap: citation support reaches 0.9900 while answer accuracy is 0.3700. Better evidence retrieval is necessary but not sufficient for better medical QA.
