KCH-MedRank improves top-10 evidence retrieval over strong baselines including a true biomedical cross-encoder, and the ablation shows this improvement has a structure worth understanding.

The flat biomedical knowledge LTR baseline is very competitive. It matches the full model at $k{=}10$. This could look like bad news for the hypergraph. We read it as informative: the result shows that flat biomedical knowledge features---MeSH hierarchy signals, entity overlap metrics, PrimeKG relations---already capture most of the ranking signal that the full model exploits. The hypergraph adds statistically significant benefit at $k{=}5$, in questions without direct MeSH overlap, and in multi-evidence scenarios. These are exactly the conditions where pairwise similarity and flat concept features fall short. The hypergraph is a targeted structural component whose effect surfaces where indirect knowledge pathways are needed, but it does not add uniformly to aggregate top-10 metrics.

A notable finding is that removing domain-grounded concept annotations from the hypergraph degrades performance relative to the flat knowledge baseline. This suggests that medical constraints are necessary for productive graph-based biomedical reranking. However, we acknowledge a design limitation: the current ablation compares conditions that differ in both structure (presence/absence of graph) and feature set (presence/absence of medical knowledge features), so it does not cleanly isolate whether raw topology itself is harmful or simply lacks the signal that medical annotations provide. The no-medical-knowledge hypergraph is not uniformly worse than retrieval-only LTR (it improves Recall@10 and nDCG@10 while losing on MRR@10), which further complicates a simple ``raw topology hurts'' interpretation. A future $2 \times 2$ factorial design (with/without graph $\times$ with/without medical knowledge) would provide stronger evidence. What the current results do establish is that domain-grounded biomedical annotations are the primary driver of ranking improvement, and that graph topology alone, built without such annotations, is insufficient.

The interpretability analysis categorizes 648 of 661 rescued passages by which knowledge mechanism is most strongly activated. MeSH hierarchy paths account for 75.9\%, shared entity clusters for 23.9\%, and PrimeKG relations for the remainder. A methodological caveat applies: this classification identifies which features show the highest activation values during rescues, not which causally drive the ranking decisions. SHAP~\citep{lundberg2017unified} or permutation-based feature attribution would strengthen the causal claim in future work. 13 of the 661 rescued passages could not be cleanly mapped to a single dominant mechanism. That 89.2\% of rescues involve multiple concurrent knowledge pathways, and that 39.5\% activate all three, suggests that the constraints work as an integrated block rather than as isolated heuristics---consistent with the ablation finding that individual feature groups are highly redundant.

On the practical side, the speed difference matters. KCH-MedRank's test-time feature construction and LightGBM scoring takes 37.59 seconds on CPU for 943 questions against 699.46 seconds on CUDA for the MedCPT Cross-Encoder, an $18.6\times$ gap when candidate generation and offline training are excluded (Table~\ref{tab:efficiency-analysis} and Figure~\ref{fig:efficiency-pareto}). This is a reranking-stage latency comparison under cached features, not an end-to-end system comparison. The gain comes from using precomputed dense biomedical semantic features rather than online cross-encoder scoring. We note that this comparison is between a knowledge-augmented tabular reranker and a neural pairwise reranker operating on the same candidate pool, not a pure model-capacity comparison; KCH-MedRank benefits from additional MeSH, entity, and PrimeKG resources that the Cross-Encoder does not use. A full knowledge-constrained reranker operating at this throughput is practical for large-scale biomedical evidence retrieval.

\paragraph{Generalization.} Supplementary diagnostics on PubMedQA and BEIR NFCorpus provide preliminary evidence on transfer. On PubMedQA, the lightweight HGB knowledge reranker improves Recall@10 over Hybrid RRF. On NFCorpus, retrieval-feature LambdaMART improves nDCG@10 and Recall@10. These results suggest that supervised reranking with retrieval features generalizes beyond BioASQ, and that biomedical knowledge features may extend to other biomedical QA formats. However, full KCH-MedRank generalization remains to be tested: PubMedQA used a lightweight HGB reranker rather than the complete BioASQ pipeline, and NFCorpus lacks MeSH, entity, PrimeKG, and hypergraph features entirely. Generalizing the full knowledge-augmented architecture to new datasets requires building dataset-specific knowledge features, which we leave to future work.

Several limitations deserve acknowledgment. The method requires a strong candidate pool; low top-100 recall imposes a hard ceiling no reranker can overcome. Exact-name PrimeKG mapping remains sparse and limits relation-based feature coverage. The flat knowledge LTR baseline is highly competitive, so hypergraph features add value in specific conditions rather than uniformly. The three-mechanism interpretability analysis identifies which features activate during rescues, not which causally drive ranking decisions. SHAP or comparable attribution would strengthen the causal claim. Hyperparameter sensitivity was measured on a 30-question sample at the correlation level; full retrieval-metric stability under the complete LambdaMART pipeline has not been systematically verified.

\paragraph{Concept Normalization: Scope and Limits.}
The error analysis identifies synonym and abbreviation failures as the largest remaining gap. We address this with a two-pronged response: a lightweight synonym-aware feature augmentation integrated into KCH-MedRank (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and a quantitative synonym-coverage analysis of the remaining gap.

The normalization module bridges a measurable fraction of synonym failures. On the test set, 18.6\% of question--gold-passage pairs have zero exact entity overlap that becomes non-zero after normalized variant expansion. On the validation split, the synonym-aware features yield MRR@10 $+0.0031$, Recall@10 $+0.0056$, and nDCG@10 $+0.0090$ over the base flat-knowledge configuration (Figure~\ref{fig:synonym-normalization}). The gains are concentrated in the zero-overlap subset identified in error analysis.

However, the normalization module has structural limits. The 213,754 MeSH entry terms from the NLM 2026 release cover clinical and standard biochemical terminology but do not include all ad-hoc abbreviations and variant nomenclatures used in the primary biomedical literature. Specifically: (i) novel drug compound names (e.g., DITPA as a non-standard abbreviation for 3,5-diiodothyropropionic acid) are absent from MeSH entry terms because MeSH descriptors are curated for indexing, not for exhaustive surface-form coverage; (ii) post-translational modification variants (e.g., mono-ubiquitinated vs.\ poly-ubiquitinated LDH) are not distinct MeSH descriptors but rather modifiers on a shared parent concept; (iii) rare disease terminology (e.g., GIST, Fanconi anemia subtypes) has limited MeSH entry-term depth. These cases require concept-level normalization that maps surface forms to unified concept identifiers (UMLS CUIs), which is beyond the scope of the current text-level variant expansion.

We therefore recommend that future biomedical retrieval systems treat concept normalization as a pre-computation step---building a mapping from surface forms to UMLS concept unique identifiers at indexing time~\citep{bodenreider2004umls,leaman2013dnorm}---rather than as a post-hoc reranking feature. This would bridge the cases where even MeSH entry terms fall short. For the present work, the lightweight module demonstrates that even partial synonym handling produces measurable ranking improvements, and we quantify both the gain and the remaining gap.

Scaling the PubMedQA generation pilot to include a completed biomedical cross-encoder condition would strengthen the downstream evidence. Extending NFCorpus with dataset-specific knowledge features would give a more complete robustness picture. The current PubMedQA pilot (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-generation}) serves as a diagnostic, not as a full RAG evaluation: it uses 100 questions, one prompt template, and a single LLM, and it measures answer accuracy rather than citation faithfulness, unsupported claims, or answer-evidence consistency. The 0.9900 citation support alongside 0.3700 answer accuracy indicates that better evidence retrieval is necessary but not sufficient for better medical QA.
